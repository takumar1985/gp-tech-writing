I Spent 25 Years Inside the World's Biggest Banks. Here's What Actually Goes Wrong.

By Timothy A., Consulting Technical Director at Greenwich Pacific Technology Partners

Twenty-five years. That's how long I've been building, fixing and occasionally firefighting technology inside banks most people have heard of. UK, Europe, North America, the Middle East, Asia-Pacific. I've sat in the war rooms, written the incident reports, and stood in front of regulators explaining why things broke.

People assume bank technology fails because of bad engineers. It doesn't. It fails because of decisions made eighteen months earlier in a budget meeting nobody remembers.

The pattern is almost always the same. A programme gets funded, scoped, and then quietly squeezed. The contingency disappears first. Then the test environments. Then someone asks whether we really need to rehearse the failover. The launch goes ahead anyway because the date was announced to the board. Eight months later there's a Sev 1, and everyone acts surprised.

I've watched a payments platform go live with a documented, signed-off single point of failure because fixing it meant a six-week slip and nobody wanted to own that conversation. It held for a year. Then it didn't. The post-incident review ran to forty pages and the root cause was one line: we knew.

The other thing that goes wrong is quieter. Knowledge walks out the door. The engineer who actually understood the message flows retires, or gets made redundant in a cost round, and suddenly nobody can tell you why that reconciliation job runs at 02:17 and not 02:00. I've been that engineer called back in at consulting rates to explain systems to the people who replaced me.

None of this is mysterious. Good bank technology is boring. Redundant. Tested. Documented by people who are still employed. The drama comes from skipping the boring parts.

If you're running a payments, cloud or resilience programme right now and any of this sounds familiar, it usually means the boring parts got skipped somewhere upstream. Worth a conversation before the incident, not after.
